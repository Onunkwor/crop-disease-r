1. Reading disease data

How much disease is there, and where did it come from?

The question

Cassava mosaic disease is caused by viruses that turn cassava leaves into a yellow-green patchwork and stunt the plant. It reaches a field in two ways:

  • in the planting material, when farmers plant stem cuttings taken from infected plants, or
  • by whiteflies, which carry the virus from plant to plant as they feed.

In 2015 and 2017, researchers from the Central and West African Virus Epidemiology (WAVE) programme’s hub at Covenant University walked 512 cassava fields across 13 states in South-West and North-Central Nigeria and recorded what they saw. Their data are free for anyone to use. Today we use them to answer two questions: how much disease was there, and where did it come from?

First steps in R (15 minutes)

If you have never used R, start here. Type each line into the console and press Enter.

R is a calculator:

2 + 3
[1] 5

You can store a result under a name with <-, and use the name later:

plants <- 100
diseased <- 40
diseased / plants
[1] 0.4

A function does a job. You give it something in brackets, and it gives an answer back:

heights <- c(12, 15, 9, 20)   # c() combines numbers into a list
mean(heights)
[1] 14
max(heights)
[1] 20

A table of data is called a data frame. Each row is one thing (here, one field) and each column is one measurement. You will meet one in a moment.

Packages add extra tools. You install them once (see Get set up), and switch them on each session with library().

That is all you need for today.

Load the data

library(readxl)
library(dplyr)
library(ggplot2)

field <- read_excel("data/cassava_mosaic_survey.xlsx", sheet = "Field")
nrow(field)
[1] 512
names(field)
 [1] "Year"               "Field"              "Country"           
 [4] "State"              "Altitude"           "Mean_CMD_Severity" 
 [7] "CMD_Incidence"      "Cutting_Infection"  "Whitefly_Infection"
[10] "Total_whitefly"

Each row is one field. The columns we need:

Column What it means
Year 2015 or 2017
State the state the field is in
CMD_Incidence the share of plants in the field showing symptoms, from 0 to 1
Mean_CMD_Severity how bad the symptoms were, on a 1 to 5 scale, averaged over the diseased plants
Cutting_Infection the share of the infection that came in with the planting material
Whitefly_Infection the share that was carried in by whiteflies
Total_whitefly how many whiteflies were counted in the field

Incidence and severity are different things

These two words get mixed up all the time, so it is worth being precise:

  • Incidence asks how many plants are diseased. A field with 40 sick plants out of 100 has an incidence of 0.4.
  • Severity asks how badly a plant is affected. A plant can be diseased but only mildly.

A field can have high incidence and low severity (many plants, mild symptoms), or the reverse. You need both to describe an outbreak.

Cassava mosaic severity is scored on a 1 to 5 scale, from 1 (no symptoms) to 5 (severe mosaic, distorted leaves and a stunted plant). The numbers are ordered categories, not measurements: a score of 4 is worse than a 2, but it is not “twice as bad”. Keep that in mind whenever you average scores like these.

How much disease was there?

field |>
  group_by(Year) |>
  summarise(
    fields         = n(),
    mean_incidence = mean(CMD_Incidence),
    mean_severity  = mean(Mean_CMD_Severity, na.rm = TRUE)
  )
# A tibble: 2 × 4
   Year fields mean_incidence mean_severity
  <dbl>  <int>          <dbl>         <dbl>
1  2015    184          0.438          2.73
2  2017    328          0.122          2.15

In 2015, on average 44% of plants in a field showed symptoms. By 2017 that had fallen to about 12%.

A picture shows the spread across fields, not just the average:

ggplot(field, aes(x = factor(Year), y = CMD_Incidence)) +
  geom_boxplot(fill = "#d8e9d0") +
  labs(x = "Survey year", y = "Share of plants with symptoms",
       title = "Cassava mosaic incidence in 512 Nigerian fields") +
  theme_minimal()

Where did the infection come from?

The route of infection is only recorded for fields that had some disease, so we keep just those:

diseased <- field |> filter(CMD_Incidence > 0)
nrow(diseased)
[1] 360
summary(diseased$Cutting_Infection)
   Min. 1st Qu.  Median    Mean 3rd Qu.    Max. 
 0.0000  0.7088  0.9091  0.8070  1.0000  1.0000

The median field got about 91% of its infection from the planting material. Let us count how many fields got at least half of it that way, and how many got more than half:

diseased |>
  summarise(
    fields              = n(),
    at_least_half       = mean(Cutting_Infection >= 0.5),
    more_than_half      = mean(Cutting_Infection > 0.5)
  )
# A tibble: 1 × 3
  fields at_least_half more_than_half
   <int>         <dbl>          <dbl>
1    360           0.9           0.85

In nine out of ten diseased fields, at least half of the disease was planted, not caught, and in 85% it was more than half. Notice how much the wording matters: “at least half” and “more than half” give different numbers, because 18 fields sit at exactly 50%. That changes what a farmer should do: the first defence is clean planting material, not spraying for whiteflies.

NoteExercise 2

Do fields with more whiteflies get more of their infection from whiteflies? Make a plot of Total_whitefly against Whitefly_Infection for the diseased fields.

TipAnswer 
ggplot(diseased, aes(x = Total_whitefly + 1, y = Whitefly_Infection)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.4) +
  scale_x_log10() +
  labs(x = "Whiteflies counted (+1, log scale)",
       y = "Share of infection from whiteflies") +
  theme_minimal()
cor(diseased$Total_whitefly, diseased$Whitefly_Infection, method = "spearman")
[1] 0.2969913

There is a link, but a loose one. The whitefly count is one visit on one day, while the symptoms record the whole season. A single count is a snapshot of something that keeps changing.

What to take away

  • Always ask how many (incidence) and how badly (severity) separately.
  • Treat 1 to 5 scores as ordered categories, not measurements.
  • A survey can tell you not only how much disease there is, but where it came from, and that decides what farmers should do about it.
